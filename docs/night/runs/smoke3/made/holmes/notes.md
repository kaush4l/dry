# Notes: Sherlock Holmes

All pages read 2026-11-23 and saved in sources/.

## Who created Sherlock Holmes
- Holmes is a fictional detective made by the British author Arthur Conan Doyle (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- The Arthur Conan Doyle Encyclopedia also names Conan Doyle as the creator and dates the character to 1887 (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- EBSCO's research starter likewise credits Sir Arthur Conan Doyle (source: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-appearance-sherlock-holmes/, read 2026-11-23)
- In the draft, Doyle first called the detective Sherrinford Holmes and the narrator Ormond Sacker, then renamed them before publication (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet, read 2026-11-23)

## Title of the first story
- Holmes first appears in the novel A Study in Scarlet, which is also Watson's first appearance (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia calls it the first Holmes novel; its working title was A Tangled Skein, and it was written in about six weeks in March-April 1886 (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet, read 2026-11-23)
- EBSCO agrees that A Study in Scarlet is his first appearance (source: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-appearance-sherlock-holmes/, read 2026-11-23)

## Year and venue of first publication
- It was first printed in 1887 in the magazine Beeton's Christmas Annual (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet, read 2026-11-23)
- Wikipedia gives the year 1887 for Holmes's first appearance in print (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia lists the first printing as Beeton's Christmas Annual, 21 November 1887, published by Ward, Lock & Co.; this disagrees with EBSCO's 1 December 1887 (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/A_Study_in_Scarlet, read 2026-11-23)
- EBSCO dates the publication in Beeton's Christmas Annual to 1 December 1887; this disagrees with the Doyle Encyclopedia's 21 November 1887 (source: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-appearance-sherlock-holmes/, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia's page on the magazine puts it in November 1887, which supports the November date; it adds that Ward, Lock & Co. paid £25 for the copyright (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Beeton%27s_Christmas_Annual, read 2026-11-23)
- The year, 1887, and the venue, Beeton's Christmas Annual, are the same in every source; only the day and month differ.

## Speech trait 1: he explains his reasoning aloud, step by step, as a lesson
- Holmes's speech sets out his reasoning in order: he states the obvious facts, draws what follows, and asks himself rhetorical questions to show how he thinks and to teach Watson and the reader (source: https://www.shmoop.com/study-guides/sherlock-holmes/writing-style.html, read 2026-11-23)
- Shmoop calls Holmes the character who talks the most, and contrasts his careful account of his reasoning with other characters' emotional, slangy testimony (source: https://www.shmoop.com/study-guides/sherlock-holmes/writing-style.html, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia, drawing on the canon, describes him as egotistical and having didactic manners (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)

## Speech trait 2: maxims, and calling his conclusions "elementary"
- Holmes states his method as general rules, for example that it is a mistake to theorize before having data, and that once the impossible is ruled out what remains must be true (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- He often calls his conclusions "elementary", but the exact line "Elementary, my dear Watson" never appears in Doyle's sixty stories; the nearest is in "The Crooked Man" (1893) (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- unconfirmed: the full catchphrase is usually credited to William Gillette's 1899 stage play, but Wikipedia says this is only alleged and appears in some versions of the script, not others (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia quotes him on keeping emotion out of reasoning, putting the head before the heart (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- Only Wikipedia names the maxims as a speech habit; the Doyle page supports it indirectly. Two more searches and two reads (403) found no third page.

## Speech trait 3: dry irony toward Watson, and the address "my dear Watson"
- Holmes sometimes addresses his friend as "my dear Watson" (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
- In "How Watson Learned the Trick" (1924), Holmes answers Watson's attempt at deduction with mock praise, then tells him gently, as "my dear Watson" and "my dear fellow", that his deductions are wrong (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/How_Watson_Learned_the_Trick, read 2026-11-23)
- The Doyle Encyclopedia says his humour was often wry irony or outright sarcasm, his habitual attitude half-humorous and half-cynical, and his manner seldom effusive (source: https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes, read 2026-11-23)
